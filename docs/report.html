2 · The problem

Main challenges

The task sounds simple: per frame, who has the ball. Seven things make it hard. Each one shaped a part of the pipeline.

1 · The ball is often missing

Missing on 40.7% of graded frames, and in long gaps (median 10.7 s, up to 105 s), often during tracker blackouts that also lose the players. A rule that needs the ball says nothing for 10–30 s at a time. → ball-free context features (§15).

2 · Players have no identity

46 anonymous slots for 22 players, with a team but no ID. Slots fragment (11.5 tracklets per minute) and swap. Nothing can follow "the player on the ball" for long. → team-level features only.

3 · Possession is a rule, not a sensor

A loose ball stays with the last team; touches, deflections and duels without control do not change possession. "Nearest player" is wrong exactly there. → touch detector (§5).

4 · The metric punishes fragmentation

spellF1 matches whole possessions by overlap. A 3-frame flicker cuts a possession in two and adds a false one; a missed 0.8 s possession merges three into one. → change penalties in a decoder (§14).

5 · Almost no labels for this match

Only 5 public minutes (17 possessions): one miss moves the score by ~6 points, and it ranks the organisers' own baselines in the wrong order. → a test bench on 7 public labelled matches (§10).

6 · The public data defines possession differently

Our training labels (DFL) change possession about twice as often as the hackathon's: 8.4 against 4.3 per live minute. → the change rate is set on the real match's possession rate, never fitted to DFL.

7 · We may not look at the data

The terms forbid giving the match data to a cloud LLM. Code is written from the format docs; only aggregates come back. → a degrader that makes public data look like the real tracking (§11).

5 · Current work · direction 2

A touch detector: who really plays the ball

M2's team evidence is "a player within 1.5 m of the ball". That cannot tell a control from a pass flying past an opponent, a duel or a deflection, which is where the hand-over losses are. The ball's velocity can (possession/touches.py):

Control

A player of one team is within 1.5 m of the ball and moves with it (relative speed under 2 m/s) for at least 0.4 s. A pass flying by has the ball's full speed relative to the player, so it never qualifies, however close it passes.

Deflection

Next to a player, a fast ball turns by more than 30° and that team does not then control it. It must not change possession.

Contact

Any other sharp change of the ball's velocity (over 3 m/s in 0.24 s) next to a player: kicks, blocks, clearances, one-touch interceptions.

Team-level, not slot-level: a control episode is "the nearest player moving with the ball belongs to team T" on consecutive frames, whichever slot that is. The detections become 10 features for the model: which team last and next controlled or touched the ball, and how long ago or until.

Checked against real events

The DFL matches come with official event logs (9,961 events), synced to the tracking frames (passes agree with the possession label 98% of the time). They tell us whether the detector sees what a human logger saw:

A bench bug found on the wayOn degraded tracking the first detector found no controls at all. The cause was the degrader: it added independent noise on every frame, giving a frame-to-frame jitter of 58 cm for players and 88 cm for the ball. The real tracking jitters by 0.1 cm and 0.9 cm: its errors are slow offsets that drift over a second or two. The degrader now adds smooth noise of the same size (1.2 cm and 2.2 cm), and a test pins it to the real data.

Since then: three new pieces, none adopted yet

All three are built from open data only. The hackathon match is kept for a last fine-tuning step, never as the base.

Carrier model

For every tracked player, the probability that he has the ball (possession/carrier.py). Its labels come from clean tracking, so they don't depend on either dataset's definition of possession. With the ball missing it finds the right player 58% of the time (best simple rule: 40%) and the right team 77% (74%).

Path decoder

Follows the ball from player to player (possession/path.py): possession changes only when the other team receives the ball; a ball in flight keeps its last team, so deflections change nothing. Built and tested, but on the bench it loses to M2 by 0.015 (1 of 7 matches), and it adds hand-overs on the real match.

Ball filling

Where the ball is during a gap, with an error radius (possession/ballfill.py). In gaps over 10 s it is off by 9.6 m against 15–18 m for simple rules, and its 80% radius covers 77% of true errors. On its own, though, the nearest player to the filled ball is no guide to the team (50%).

6 · Plan

Next steps

  1. Fix the yardstick. The hackathon labels are computed by a rule from corrected tracking, and DFL gives clean tracking. So relabel DFL with a hackathon-style rule: possession changes only on a real control by the other team, and a loose ball stays with the last team. Tune it until the relabelled matches show the public labels' statistics: about 4.3 possessions per live minute, median 7.6 s, 10% under 2.2 s. Then train and judge on those labels.
  2. Re-judge on that yardstick M2 + touches, the carrier features, and the path decoder used narrowly: move each of M2's own changes onto the frame of the control, adding no changes of its own.
  3. Varied degradation in training: several noise draws, with gap length, blackouts and noise drawn around the calibrated values, so the real tracker falls inside what the models have seen. Also measure ball-speed spikes on the real tracking, to spot false ball detections the degrader never simulates.
  4. Who touched it last before a stoppage (up to +0.11), from the touch detections.
  5. Ball detection in the tactical video, run locally: the only way to add information inside the gaps (+0.17 ceiling). The biggest effort; also PFF World Cup 2022 data for more gap examples.
  6. Fine-tuning on the hackathon match as the last step: carrier and ball filling (ballfill.finetune, written, not run).
  7. Tech doc AndaAnda.md for the submission, built from this report.

7 · The task

What we predict

The tactical video has 199,319 frames at 25 per second. For each one we write H, A, D or X (abstain). Only the two halves are graded: frames 11,185–90,651 and 110,808–190,197, so 158,857 frames.

The ground truth is mechanical: Metrica's corrected tracking flags the ball dead or names the team that owns it, frame by frame, with no smoothing. A possession is a maximal run of H or of A. A dead-ball stretch ends one, so H H D D H H counts as two possessions.

8 · The metric

How it is scored: spellF1

The board ranks on spellF1, which treats each possession as one object rather than hundreds of frames. It answers a coach's question: did you find the possessions that happened, with roughly the right start and end?

  1. Extract the possessions (maximal H/A runs) from the truth and from the prediction.
  2. For every same-team pair that overlaps, compute the temporal IoU.
  3. Match greedily, best IoU first, one-to-one: each true possession can be credited to at most one predicted one.
  4. At each threshold τ, a pair counts as a hit if IoU ≥ τ. Compute F1, then average over τ = 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7.
IoU(p,g)=|p∩g||p∪g|F1τ=2TP2TP+FP+FNspellF1=15∑τ∈{.3,…,.7}F1τ An abstention X is first replaced by the previous label in the same half, so it means "repeat my last answer": free inside a possession, costly across a hand-over.

Three consequences shaped every design decision that follows:

Merges cost more than splits

Swallowing a 0.8 s possession between two long ones turns three true possessions into one predicted one. That one can only match one of them, and only at the lower thresholds. A split costs about 1.4 possessions.

Flicker is fatal

Every spurious blip is a false positive and also cuts a true possession in two. The raw nearest-player rule invents several times more possessions than a match contains, and scores 0.05.

Timing matters, up to a point

One frame late is free; 8 frames late gives 0.89; one second late gives 0.65. Short possessions are the most fragile: a 20-frame spell shifted by 8 frames drops to IoU 0.43.

The public 5-minute window has 17 possessions; one miss moves spellF1 by about 6 points, and the organisers showed it picks the wrong smoothing window even for the baseline. A method needs many labelled full matches to be judged. The bench supplies them, in conditions that look like the hackathon's.

11 · Simulation

The degrader: making clean tracking look automatic

DFL's tracking is corrected and complete: the ball is always there, players never vanish. Scoring on that would flatter every method. bench/degrade.py damages it so its statistics match the hackathon's, while leaving the labels untouched.

Ball gaps as a point process

Each frame can start a gap with a probability that depends on the situation. Gap length is drawn from a mixture: 11% short flickers, 89% lognormal (median 9.5 s, σ 0.75). Overlapping gaps merge (their union is what is missing).

P(gap starts at t)=hstate(t)·(1+a·crowdt)·(1+b·𝟙vball>12) The two base rates halive and hdead are found by bisection so that the ball is missing on 19% of live frames and 61% of dead ones. The real split was estimated from mean player speed: the hackathon match and DFL have near-identical speed distributions, and walking pace is mostly dead ball in both.

Players: blackouts, tracklets, slots

  • Blackouts: 30% of ball gaps also hide each player with a probability drawn from U(0.2, 0.6), plus rare player-only blackouts. This reproduces "≤ 18 tracked ⇒ ball missing 85%".
  • Tracklets: each player's visible time is cut at identity breaks (mean every 400 s) and at hides. Independent single-player dropouts are rare (0.5%), because the real tracker rarely drops one player alone.
  • Slots: tracklets are packed into anonymous slots, a free slot of the same team first. 2% land in a wrong-team slot (cross-team identity switch).
  • Position noise: a smooth offset that drifts over about a second (white noise passed three times through a 1 s moving average), not independent per-frame noise. The real tracking's frame-to-frame jitter is tiny (0.1 cm for players, 0.9 cm for the ball); white noise of the same size gave 58–88 cm and made every velocity meaningless (§5).

13 · Method 0

Baseline: smooth50

The organisers' reference: on each frame where the ball is tracked, give it to the team of the nearest tracked player (distances in metres, both axes scaled). Where the ball is not tracked, say nobody. Then take a centred majority vote over ±50 frames (4 s); D if the window saw no ball at all.

  • Its only idea of a dead ball is "no ball seen": wrong both ways (dead balls are often tracked, live ones often lost). Dead-ball frame error: 51%.
  • A fixed window blurs each change by up to 2 s and outvotes any possession shorter than about 2 s.
  • There is no notion of a possession lasting: the vote is recomputed frame by frame.
14 · Method 1

M1: evidence + Viterbi decoding

A hidden Markov model treats the true state (H, A or D) as hidden and changing rarely, observed only through noisy clues. Decoding means finding the single sequence of states that best explains all the clues at once. We use it in its "energy" form: per-frame evidence scores et(s) and a cost c(s′,s) for each change.

s*=arg⁡maxs1:T∑t=1Tet(st)−∑t=2Tc(st−1,st) δt(s)=et(s)+maxs′[δt−1(s′)−c(s′,s)] Viterbi computes δt(s), the best score of any sequence ending in state s at frame t, one frame at a time, keeping a back-pointer. Walking the back-pointers from the end gives the optimum. Cost: O(T·S²), that is 3 × 3 operations per frame, under a second per match. It is exact: the tests check it against brute force over all 36 sequences.

Why this beats a vote: a change is made only if the evidence for it, summed over the frames it covers, exceeds its cost. A 3-frame flicker earns about 6 points against a cost of 40: ignored. An opponent controlling the ball for 1 s earns about 50: taken. When there is no evidence, staying put is cheapest, so the last team carries forward through gaps on its own. And because the whole half is decoded at once, each change lands where the evidence flips, with no window lag.

The evidence (hand-built, no training)

  • Dead vs alive: mean player speed over 2 s against a walking threshold of 1.5 m/s, a strong bonus when the ball is beyond a line, and a small one when the ball is not tracked.
  • Home vs away: only when a player is within 1.5 m of the ball, the gap between the two teams' nearest distances. A ball in flight or invisible gives no team evidence rather than a wrong guess.

15 · Method 2

M2: learned evidence with gradient-boosted trees

M1's clues all need the ball. In the real data the ball is missing for 10–30 s at a time, and a hand-written rule cannot tell slow live play from a stoppage. M2 replaces the hand-built evidence with a model that combines many weak clues into calibrated probabilities P(H),P(A),P(D) per frame, then decodes them with the same Viterbi.

42 context features, mostly ball-free

Team shape

Per team: centroid, forward speed, spread, front and back lines, in that team's attacking direction. The attacking team pushes up, the defending team drops back.

Tempo

Mean player speed over 1, 3 and 8 s, and "past 3 s minus next 3 s": a sudden team-wide stop is the signature of a whistle.

Ball memory

Seconds since and until the ball is seen, where it was last and will next be seen (distance to a line), and its last speed: a ball last seen heading for a line explains a gap.

Control memory

Which team last and next had a player within 1.5 m of the ball, and how long ago. Plus the M1 team clue averaged over 0.5, 2 and 5 s.

How gradient-boosted trees learn

The model is a sum of many small decision trees. Each new tree is fitted to the errors of the sum so far, more precisely to the negative gradient of the loss, and added with a small learning rate. For three classes there are three such sums, turned into probabilities by a softmax and trained on the multiclass log loss:

Fk(m)(x)=Fk(m−1)(x)+ηhk,m(x)pk=eFk∑jeFjL=−logpy hk,m is a tree fitted to −∂L/∂Fk = 𝟙[y = k] − pk. Trees handle mixed scales, missing values (NaN goes to a learned side of each split) and interactions such as "ball unseen and players slow and last seen near a line" without any feature engineering.

Why LightGBM: it bins features into histograms and grows trees leaf-wise, so a model over 900,000 rows trains in about 30 s on a laptop. It handles NaN natively. And it can be made fully deterministic (fixed seed, row-wise, fixed threads), which reproducibility needs. Settings: 300 trees, 31 leaves, learning rate 0.05, at least 200 rows per leaf.

Training without leaking

  • Training data: the 7 DFL matches degraded exactly as on the bench, so the model learns from hackathon-like gaps.
  • Every match is also used with home and away swapped (teams and labels), so the model cannot learn "home usually has the ball" and gets twice the data.
  • One frame in three is kept: neighbouring frames are near duplicates.
  • Leave-one-match-out: 7 extra models, each trained without one match. When the bench scores a DFL match, it automatically uses the model that never saw it.

Probabilities enter the decoder as et(s)=logP(s∣xt), so only the penalty-to-evidence ratio matters. With confident evidence the switch penalty could drop to 10: held-out DFL is flat for 3–10, and 10 gives 4.16 possessions per live minute on the real match. The two criteria now agree.

16 · Method 3 · evaluated, not adopted

M3: the semi-Markov decoder

M2 still splits live possessions inside long ball gaps. Its probabilities are not wrong: a long gap usually is a stoppage, so P(dead) rises with gap length (0.30 for 10–15 s, 0.65 beyond 60 s). The decoder is the problem. It adds up the same slight lean over hundreds of near-identical frames, as if each were new information, and the sum outweighs the cost of stopping and restarting.

A hidden semi-Markov model scores whole segments instead of frames, which gives two natural places to fix this:

score([a,b),s)=∑t=ab−1wtet(s)+λlogPs(b−a)wt=min(1,κG) δc(s)=maxd≤Ds[maxs′(δc−d(s′)−c(s′,s))+score([c−d,c),s)] Segment evidence comes from prefix sums, so each candidate duration costs O(1). G is the length of the ball gap a frame belongs to.

Duration priors

Lognormal per state: stoppages from DFL (median 18 s, σ 0.92, consistent with the public window); possessions wide (σ 1.2) with a tunable median, because DFL and the hackathon disagree there.

Gap weighting

A ball gap of G frames counts as at most κ frames of evidence: its frames repeat one piece of information. A true stoppage still wins (a confident P(dead) over κ frames beats the penalties); a slight lean no longer does.

Speed and precision

The segment search runs on 0.2 s blocks (up to 3 minutes per possession, 10 per stoppage), then every boundary is moved to the exact frame, ±10 frames, from the per-frame evidence. About 3 s per match.

Result: a useful negative

The ablation (scripts/ablate_m3.py) compared 17 variants on held-out DFL, the public window, the real possession rate and the targeted scenarios. None beat M2, so M2 stays.

What we learnedThe redundancy assumption was wrong. Inside a ball gap the learned evidence is not one repeated fact: who last and next controlled the ball, and how the teams move, tell the model when a hand-over happens. Down-weighting gaps barely helped the hold case (23% → 30%) and wiped out change detection (25% → 4%). The duration priors were already implied by the learned probabilities plus the switch penalty. The 23% hold rate is a trade-off the data supports (long gaps usually are stoppages), so the next gain must come from better gap features, not from the decoder. possession/hsmm.py stays in the repo, tested against brute force, as an option for later.

18 · Method

Validation discipline

  • Noise bands. Full-match spellF1 differences under ~0.04 are noise (the sample is the possessions, not the frames); macroF1 under ~0.01. Every decision above clears that by a wide margin.
  • The public window is a correctness check, not a leaderboard. 17 possessions; it ranks the organisers' own baselines in the wrong order.
  • Held-out only. Every DFL number for a learned model comes from a model that never saw that match.
  • Definition gap. Anything that sets how often possession changes is checked against the real match's possession rate, never fitted to DFL.
  • Targeted scenarios. A global gain can hide a regression on restarts or gaps; the 13 scenarios catch it.
19 · Delivery

Submission, reproducibility and privacy

uv run python -m possession.submit rebuilds the match from the raw bundle, runs the chosen method, writes submissions/AndaAnda.csv and runs the organisers' validator. It takes about 15 s and is byte-identical across runs: dependencies are pinned in uv.lock, the model in models/lgbm.txt is trained deterministically on public data, and nothing is random.

Privacy ruleThe terms forbid giving the match data, or any extract, to a cloud LLM. Code is written against the documented formats. Every command on the hackathon data prints aggregates only, and frame-level files (data/, official_data/, reports/, submissions/) are gitignored. All replays and examples on this page use public DFL data or the public 5-minute ground truth.
